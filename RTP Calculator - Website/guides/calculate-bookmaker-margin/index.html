The RTP of a sports bet: definition and calculation

Published on 29 July 2026 · Updated on 25 October 2026

On any sports betting site, one number never appears on screen: the share of the stakes that each bet on each event actually gives back to players. That is what the RTP measures. It is displayed nowhere, and yet it can be computed from the odds themselves, in three mathematical operations.

The definition

The RTP (Return To Player) is the percentage of the stakes that a betting market gives back to bettors, on average. An RTP of 94% means that for every €100 staked on that market, across all outcomes, the odds pay back the equivalent of €94 in winnings: the remaining €6 is the bookmaker's margin.

Two things matter when reading it. First, the RTP is not an individual promise: on any single bet you win or you lose. It is a structural average, the one that applies to all bettors combined over time. Second, the RTP is measured market by market: every match and every bet type has its own, and the gaps are much wider than most people imagine.

The formula in three operations

1. Convert each odd into an implied probability: probability = 1 ÷ decimal odd. Odds of 2.00 are worth 50%, odds of 4.00 are worth 25%.

2. Add up the implied probabilities of every outcome of the market. If the odds were fair, the total would be exactly 100%; in practice, the total always exceeds 100%, and that excess is where the margin lives.

3. Derive the RTP: RTP = 100 ÷ total × 100. The bookmaker's margin is what is missing to reach 100%: margin = 100 − RTP.

Two-outcome example (tennis)

Player A at 1.80, player B at 1.95.

1÷1.80 = 55.6%; 1÷1.95 = 51.3%. Total: 106.8%.

RTP = 100 ÷ 106.8 × 100 = 93.6%. Bookmaker's margin: 100 − 93.6 = 6.4%.

In other words, on this match, bettors share on average €93.60 for every €100 staked, whoever wins; the remaining €6.40 goes to the operator.

Three-outcome example (football, 1X2)

Home 2.10, draw 3.40, away 3.60.

47.6% + 29.4% + 27.8% = 104.8%.

RTP = 100 ÷ 104.8 × 100 = 95.4%. Margin: 4.6%.

Whatever the number of outcomes or the odds format

The method does not change with the number of outcomes: a 30-runner outright market is computed the same way, by adding the 30 implied probabilities. The more outcomes a market has, the further the sum tends to drift from 100%, and the lower the RTP.

The formula assumes decimal (European) odds. Fractional or American odds are converted first: 6/4 becomes 2.50, +150 also becomes 2.50, −200 becomes 1.50. The guide on odds formats details these conversions, and the RTP Calculator automatically converts every odd you enter, whatever its format.

Why bookmakers never display the RTP

The margin is the price of the service. Unlike brokerage fees, it is written nowhere: it is baked into the odds, and no regulation requires displaying it market by market. Two odds of 1.85 on two different matches look equivalent; if the opposite odd is 1.85 in one case and 2.05 in the other, the first market has an RTP of 92.5% and the second 97.2%. That is exactly what the calculation makes visible: the price the bettor pays.

What makes the RTP vary

A few calculations are enough for patterns to emerge. The competition: the Premier League and other top leagues show better RTPs than lower divisions or exotic competitions, where the bookmakers' margin can be several times higher. The market type: in football, the main 1X2 is usually the most competitive market of a match; side markets (goalscorers, corners, correct scores, handicaps) often carry substantially higher margins. Timing: far from kick-off, uncertainty is higher and margins wider; they tighten as the match approaches. The event: on some very big fixtures, operators temporarily compress their margin for commercial reasons. The RTP is therefore measured market by market, not operator by operator.

A side note: in some countries the RTP is also a regulatory concept. In France, the average annual RTP of licensed online sports betting operators is capped at 85%.

What the RTP is for, day to day

Know the real price of a bet before taking it, rather than guessing it from a single odd.

Compare markets and bet types with each other: the calculation reveals systematic gaps between the 1X2 and side markets, or between a big fixture and a lower-division match.

Compare your operators on the same match: for a bet on a single outcome, what counts is of course the odd on that outcome, but computing the RTP lets you spot the operator that systematically sells its bets at a higher price.
